Welcome

What Vibration Lab Suite does

Vibration Lab Suite is a sandbox for mechanical motion. You draw a system on a canvas: balls, blocks and polygons, floor lines and ramps with their own friction, beams with pinned, roller or cantilever supports, springs, dampers, rods and hinges with motors. You choose how realistic the physics should be. Then you run it for as long as you like and watch it move, with live numbers and plots beside it.

When you are done, one button turns the whole run into an Excel workbook: every number, with units, plus charts that are already drawn. A second button records an .mp4 video.

It is built for three kinds of people:

  • Students of dynamics and vibrations, who want to see what the equations say and check their homework against a simulation.
  • Engineers who want a quick what-if model before they commit to heavy software.
  • Teachers and developers who want convincing, correct motion for demonstrations, courses and games.

Installing

There is nothing to install. Double-click VibrationLabSuite.exe. The program opens straight into the design screen with a worked example loaded.

NOTE

You need Windows 10 or 11 (64-bit) and a graphics card that supports Direct3D 11 (every PC of the last ten years does). Excel is not needed to run the program or to create the workbook. You only need Excel (or LibreOffice, Google Sheets, Numbers) to open the workbook afterwards.

1.2 Sizes and radii

Everything is measured in metres, kilograms and seconds. After placing something, select it and type exact numbers in Properties:

  • a ball has a radius;
  • a block has a width and a height;
  • a polygon has a number of sides and a radius (the distance from its centre to a corner);
  • a wedge has two leg lengths;
  • a beam has a length and a thickness, and its mass comes from the material's density;
  • a floor is a line: it has only a length and an angle.

Mass can be typed directly (kilograms) or worked out from a density and the shape's area. The computed mass and moment of inertia are shown underneath.

KEY WORD

Depth. This is a 2-D simulator. Every body is treated as 1 metre deep, so "density x area" gives its mass. If you only ever type the mass directly, depth never matters.

1.3 Floors and their friction

A floor is a line with hatching on the side it supports and no thickness: it is ground, not an object, so it can never be hinged, bent or knocked over. It has a material, and therefore friction and bounce. Place one, then select it: Properties shows its length, angle and surface material. The material sets its friction and bounciness. Give each floor its own material to compare surfaces: for example, put an ice floor and a rubber floor side by side and slide the same block across both.

1.5 Springs, dampers, rods and pins

Click a connection tool, click one body, then click another. Click near a body's centre to attach to its centre; click elsewhere on it to attach at that spot (this makes it twist as well as move).

  • A spring is born relaxed at the length you drew it. Changing its rest length in Properties pre-stretches or pre-compresses it. A positive cubic stiffness makes it harder the more it stretches.
  • A damper resists speed: force = c x (speed of stretching). It turns motion into heat. It is the only thing that dissipates energy in a pure spring-mass system.
  • A rod holds two points at a fixed distance.
  • A pin makes two bodies share one point. A pendulum is an anchor, a rod and a ball.
  • A fixed (weld) joint makes two bodies share one point and one angle: they move as one piece.

Springs change colour while the model runs: warm when they pull, cool when they push.

NOTE

Static versus kinetic friction. Static friction decides whether a resting body starts to slide: it slides only when the push along the surface exceeds the static friction times the pressing force. 2.2 The energy ledger

Energy is never created or destroyed; it only changes form. The program keeps a running account and shows it in the Energy ledger tab:

  • Work in: energy fed in by pushes and by you dragging with the mouse.
  • Dampers: heat from dampers.
  • Air drag: heat from drag.
  • Friction: heat from sliding and from rolling resistance.
  • Impacts: kinetic energy lost in collisions.

The law of energy says E(end) = E(start) + work in - energy lost. The tab prints both sides and the difference, called the numerical error. That number is the program checking itself: it should be tiny (a few thousandths of a joule at most). If it ever grows, something is wrong, and that is useful to know.

WHY?

Why does this matter? Many simulators just show motion; you cannot tell if the motion is right. A ledger that balances is strong evidence that the forces and the time stepping are correct, and it teaches the central idea of mechanics: the energy goes somewhere.

Chapter 3Running the Model

3.1 How long to run

The Run panel starts with the question How long should the model run? Type any number of seconds from a fraction of a second to an hour, or press 5 s, 10 s, 30 s, 1 min, 5 min. The model stops by itself when the time is up.

A good starting point is 100 rows per second; for a 5-minute run use 20 to 50. The panel tells you how many rows to expect. Chapter 5Worked Tutorials

5.1 1. Natural frequency and damping of a mass on a spring

Load Mass-spring-damper (1 degree of freedom) (mass 2 kg, spring 30 N/m, damper 1.5 N s/m, released from 1 m stretch).

  1. Press Compute the whole run now (fast).
  2. Open Telemetry, Spectrum, choose the channel Mass dx, press Compute spectrum. The peak reads 0.61 Hz; the tab also prints the damping ratio estimated from the decay: 0.097.
  3. Theory: natural frequency sqrt(k/m) / (2 pi) = sqrt(15) / (2 pi) = 0.616 Hz. Damping ratio = c / (2 sqrt(k m)) = 1.5 / (2 sqrt(60)) = 0.097.
  4. Now double the spring to 60 N/m in Properties and repeat: the frequency should rise by a factor of about 1.41.

5.2 2. Resonance

Same system, switched to zero gravity. Select the mass and set a harmonic push: 5 N, direction 0, frequency 3.9 rad/s (the natural frequency). Run for 30 seconds and look at Plots and the Energy ledger: the amplitude grows until the energy fed in each cycle equals the heat the damper removes. Now set the push frequency to 8 rad/s: the same push barely moves it. Switch dampers off (press Ideal) at resonance and the amplitude grows without limit.

5.3 3. Which blocks slide?

Load Friction on an incline: four materials. The ramp is 28 degrees, so tan(28 degrees) = 0.53. A block slides only when the static friction between it and the ramp is below 0.53. With a wooden ramp the combined values are rubber 0.71, steel 0.61, wood 0.50, ice 0.22, so rubber and steel stay and wood and ice slide. Run it and confirm, then check the Energy ledger: the "friction" bar is the heat from the two sliding blocks.

5.4 4. Bounce and restitution

Load Bouncing ball: ideal vs realistic. A rubber ball (e = 0.8) dropped from 2 m should rebound to e squared x 2 = 1.28 m, then 0.82 m, and so on. Read the peaks from the Plots tab (channel Ball dy) and compare. The ledger's impacts bar after the first bounce is m g h (1 - e squared) = 3.5 J for the default 0.5 kg ball.

5.5 5. A tuned mass damper

Load Tuned mass damper: a 10 kg main mass on a spring, pushed at its resonance, with a small 1 kg absorber attached by its own spring and damper.

  1. Run 40 seconds and look at Plots: the main mass moves very little because the absorber takes the motion.
  2. Open Frequency response and press Run frequency sweep: instead of one tall resonance peak you see two small ones either side. That split peak is the signature of a tuned absorber.
  3. Open Modal analysis and press the button: the two natural frequencies match the two peaks.

Chapter 6Analysis Tools

6.1 Spectrum

Choose a channel and press Compute spectrum. The program shows how strongly each frequency is present in that signal and marks the strongest. It removes the average first (so a constant offset is not mistaken for a very slow wave). For a damped oscillation the peak sits at the damped frequency.

6.2 Modal analysis

A system with several moving parts can vibrate in several modes. Each has its own natural frequency and a shape (who moves with whom). Press Compute natural frequencies and mode shapes and click a mode: in design mode it is animated on the canvas.

The analysis linearises the springs and dampers about the drawn pose; contacts and joints are ignored. A mode with 0 Hz means part of the system is not held by any spring and can drift.

6.3 Frequency response

Choose where to push (the input), where to measure (the output), a range of frequencies, and press Run frequency sweep. For each frequency the program runs a full steady-state simulation and reports the amplitude and the phase lag of the response. The result is the resonance curve of the system. Each frequency is a real simulation, so a sweep takes a few seconds to a minute.

7.1 Honest limits

  • It is a 2-D simulator. Bodies move in a plane and have a depth of 1 m.
  • The material values are generic textbook numbers. Real friction depends on the surface, the speed and the temperature: measure yours for engineering decisions.
  • Contacts are solved with a sequential-impulse method with a very small time step. Very high stacks, very fast thin objects and very stiff, very light parts can still misbehave; if the numbers blow up the program stops and tells you.
  • It is validated against theory, but it is not a certified engineering tool.

Chapter 11The Problem: One Engine, Infinitely Many Systems

A textbook solves one system at a time. For a mass on a spring you write one equation, m x'' + c x' + k x = F(t). For a double pendulum you derive a different, much longer pair of equations. For a quarter-car you derive a third. Each derivation is correct and each is useless for the next system. A general simulator cannot work that way, because the user decides the system after the program is written.

So the design question was:

KEY WORD

The generalization question. What single description of a mechanical system is (1) rich enough to express every system the user can draw, (2) simple enough that one piece of code can advance any of them in time, and (3) honest enough that the answer can be checked against the physics we already know?

The answer the program uses has three layers, each of which you will meet below.

Layer What it contains Where it lives
Description Bodies (shape, mass, material, starting state) and Links (springs, dampers, rods, hinges, welds, rollers) with their attachment points core/include/vlab/model.hpp, core/src/model.cpp
Equations Newton's second law for every body in the same coordinates, with the forces written once, per kind of element, not per system core/src/world.cpp: computeForces, potentialEnergy
Constraints and contact Everything that is not a smooth force: hinges, welds, rails, collisions, friction core/src/world.cpp: the impulse solver

The rest of the chapter is these three layers, in that order, followed by time stepping, energy accounting, testing, and a recipe for adding to them.

Chapter 12Choosing the Coordinates

12.1 The choice: three numbers per body, in world axes, always

Every body in the program, whatever it is, is described by the same three numbers (x, y, θ) and their rates (ẋ, ẏ, θ̇): position of its centre of mass in the world, and the angle it has turned. That is all the engine ever stores about where a body is.

The structure that holds them is, in world.hpp:

C++
struct BodyState {
    Vec2 pos;
    double angle = 0;
    Vec2 vel;
    double omega = 0;
    Rot rot;                       // cached cos/sin of angle
};
  • pos is the centre of mass in metres. The y axis points down, exactly like the screen, so gravity is a positive y force and a drawing needs no flipping.
  • angle is in radians. Because y points down, a positive angle turns the body clockwise on the screen. This single convention decides the sign of every moment, torque and torsional spring in the program.
  • vel, omega are the rates, in m/s and rad/s.
  • rot is a cache of cos θ and sin θ, so the sine and cosine of an angle are computed once per body per step and not once per use. A step may rotate the same body's attachment points hundreds of times (a spring distribution has up to 40 elements).

12.2 Why not "generalized coordinates"?

The classical way to analyse a pendulum is to choose the one number that describes it, the angle, and to derive the equation of motion for that number alone. This is the minimal-coordinate method, and it is the heart of Lagrangian textbooks. It gives the smallest possible set of equations, with no constraint forces to worry about.

The program deliberately does not do that, for four reasons.

  1. The user chooses the system at run time. A minimal coordinate set depends on how the bodies are connected. Pin two bodies together and you have removed two coordinates. Remove the pin and they come back. The program would have to re-derive its equations every time you clicked. Maximal coordinates (three per body, always) do not change when you add or remove a connection.
  2. Contacts break and form. A ball resting on a floor has two coordinates fewer than a ball in the air. A minimal-coordinate engine would need to change its coordinate set mid-run. With maximal coordinates the ball just has the same three numbers, and the floor contributes a force when they touch.
  3. Loops are easy. A crank, a connecting rod and a piston form a closed loop (the single-cylinder engine example). Minimal coordinates for a loop need a loop-closure equation that is hard to solve generally. Maximal coordinates plus a pin constraint handle any loop identically.
  4. One code path. The force on a body is the same few lines for every body.
WHY?

What it costs. Maximal coordinates mean more numbers than the system really has, and the constraints (a pin says "these two points must coincide") are extra equations that must be enforced. Section "Constraints" below is the price of this choice. It is the standard trade that every general-purpose physics engine makes, because the alternative cannot be general.

12.3 Local frames: how orientation stops mattering

The second decision is where things attach. A spring does not attach to "the right side of the block at x = 1.5"; it attaches to a point in the body's own frame: la for the first body and lb for the second. The world position of the attachment is then computed fresh every step:

pworld = pbody + R(θ) · llocal (5.1)

where R(θ) is the 2 × 2 rotation matrix. In code this is pos + rot.apply(local), and the inverse (world to local) is rot.applyInv(world - pos). Both live in math.hpp:

Why not store world coordinates for attachments? Because a rotating body would drag them out of place and every step would have to update them by hand. Local frames make the geometry invariant: the stored number never changes while the body moves.

Chapter 13Describing a System: Bodies, Links and the Model

The description layer is plain data. A Model is two lists, one of Body and one of Link, plus the environment (gravity, friction switches, time step) and the run settings. There is no behaviour in it. That is on purpose: the same Model can be saved to a file, shown in the interface, run by the engine, exported to Excel, or handed to the Unreal Engine plugin.

All connections are one structure, distinguished by a kind field. The full definition today, including the distribution and torsion fields added in version 1.2:

  • kind: Spring, Damper, Rod, Pin, Weld or Roller. Springs and dampers are smooth forces; the other four are constraints. This split is the most important idea in the engine and Section "Two kinds of connection" returns to it.
  • a, b: indices of the two bodies. A link always joins exactly two bodies. A wall or the ground is also a body (a Static or Anchor kind), so "spring to the ground" is not a special case.
  • la, lb: the attachment points in each body's local frame (equation 5.1).
  • k, k3: linear and cubic stiffness. The force is k s + k3 s³ where s is the stretch.
  • L0 and autoRest: the rest length, and a flag saying "keep the rest length equal to the length as drawn". Section "Rest lengths" explains why.
  • c: damping coefficient.
  • refAngle: the relative angle a Weld holds, and (since 1.2) the rest angle of a torsional spring.

  • dist, la2, lb2, distM (1.2): a distribution. If dist is 0 the link is one element between la and lb. If it is 1 or 2 the link is a row of elements spread from (la, lb) to (la2, lb2), with constant or linearly varying strength.
WHY?

Why one structure and not a class hierarchy? A Spring class, a Damper class and so on is the textbook object-oriented answer. It was rejected because (a) every operation (save, load, draw, edit, export) would need a virtual function per class, (b) adding a feature to several kinds at once (a distribution applies to both springs and dampers) would cross class boundaries, and (c) a plain struct with a tag is trivially copied, which the undo stack, the running world and the file saver all rely on. The cost is a few fields that mean nothing for some kinds. The code accepts that cost to keep the whole model one copyable value.

13.2 Mass properties: where m and I come from

A body's mass is either typed in or computed from density and area, and its moment of inertia about its centre of mass comes from the shape. Model::updateMass computes massKg, invMass, inertia and invI for every body. Two details matter for generality:

  • Inverse mass, not mass. The solver stores 1/m and 1/I. A fixed body (a floor, an anchor) has inverse mass zero, which makes it immovable without any special case: an impulse P changes velocity by P/m = P · invMass = 0.
  • Locks. A body on a rail (lockY, for instance) is not given a different equation. Its inverse mass along the locked axis is set to zero by the helper below, so no impulse the solver computes can ever move it that way:

Chapter 14The Equations of Motion for Any Body

14.1 Newton in maximal coordinates

For every dynamic body i the engine solves

mi ai = Fi Ii αi = τi (5.3)

where Fi is the total force at the centre of mass and τi the total moment about it. Everything the user can add is a contribution to Fi or τi: gravity, a spring, a damper, a push, a moment, a distributed load, the mouse. The function that adds them up is World::computeForces. This is where generality is won: each kind of element is written once, and it works between any two bodies, at any position, at any angle.

The Lagrangian derivation of the same equations (chapters 2 and 3 of Inside the Vibration Lab) gives, for a set of coordinates q,

M q̈ = − ∂V/∂q − ∂D/∂q̇ + Q (5.4)

with V the potential energy (springs and gravity), D the Rayleigh dissipation function (dampers) and Q the generalized forces of loads. Equation 5.3 and equation 5.4 are the same law. 5.4 tells us where each force comes from (the gradient of an energy), and that is how every new element was derived: write the energy, differentiate it with respect to the coordinates, and the force and the moment fall out.

14.2 Gravity and the potential energy

Gravity is the simplest contribution, and it is written in computeForces as one line per body. The potential energy of gravity with y pointing down is V = −m g (y − y0) (lower means less energy), so the force is −∂V/∂y = +m g, a positive y force. The reference y0 is the body's drawn position, so the energy starts at zero and the ledger (below) starts clean.

Here is the part of computeForces that handles springs and dampers, including distributions. Read it as three nested ideas: for each link, for each element of the link, compute one force and apply it to both bodies.

Go through it slowly.

  • if (l.kind != Spring && l.kind != Damper) continue; Only the smooth links are handled here. Rods, pins, welds and rollers are constraints and are handled by the impulse solver. The test is first so that most links cost almost nothing.
  • const BodyState &A = S[l.a], &B = S[l.b]; The state array S is passed in, not read from the world, because the RK4 integrator evaluates the forces at four trial states. The same function therefore serves the real state and the trial states.
  • const int N = l.distN(); The number of elements: 1 for an ordinary link, 4 to 40 for a distribution (one element per 0.15 m of span, with a floor of 4 and a ceiling of 40).
  • for (int q = 0; q < N; ++q) Every element does the same thing.
  • pla = l.la + (l.la2 - l.la) * fr; The attachment point of element q on the first body: a linear interpolation along the span. fr = (q + 0.5)/N is the midpoint of the q-th slice. The same is done for the second body, so each element runs between the two matching points.
  • ke = max(0, k + add) * span / N; The stiffness of this element. If the link stores k newtons per metre per metre of span, then a slice of width span/N has stiffness k × span/N. For a linear distribution, add = distM * sd where sd is the distance along the span, so the value per metre grows as y = m x. The max(0, ...) stops a negative slope from creating a negative stiffness (which would be a pushing spring that grows with stretch: unstable).
  • ra = A.rot.apply(pla) The lever arm: the vector from the body's centre to its attachment point, in world axes.
  • d = (B.pos + rb) - (A.pos + ra) The vector between the two attachment points in the world. len = length(d) is the spring's current length and u = d / len its direction. The guard len > 1e-9 prevents division by zero when the two points coincide; the direction is then arbitrary (1, 0), and the force is zero anyway because the stretch is far from the rest length.
  • s = len - l.L0 The stretch. The force, f = ke * s + l.k3 * s^3, is dV/ds for V = ½ k s² + ¼ k3 s⁴.
  • Damper branch. The relative velocity of the two attachment points is va = A.vel + cross(A.omega, ra), vb = B.vel + cross(B.omega, rb) (equation "v + ω × r"). Why the force is correct for any orientation: the derivation

Take a spring between point Pa = pa + R(θa) la and Pb = pb + R(θb) lb. Its length is s + L0 = |Pb − Pa|. The energy is V(s). The generalized force on any coordinate q is −(dV/ds)(∂s/∂q). The derivatives are:

∂ℓ/∂pb = u, ∂ℓ/∂pa = −u, ∂ℓ/∂θb = u · (R′ lb) = rb × u (5.5)

where rb = R lb is the lever arm and the last equality is the identity (R′ l) · u = r × u in 2-D. So the force on body b is −f u at its centre of mass and the moment is −f (rb × u) = rb × (−f u): the force acting at the attachment point. That is the entire derivation of the code above. Nothing in it depends on the angle of the spring, of either body, or of the world: only on the vectors r and u that the program computes fresh each step. This is why a spring that is vertical, horizontal, diagonal, or rotating with a tumbling body needs no special handling.

WHY?

Why not write the spring as forces along x and y and be done? Because the moment would then be forgotten. A spring attached off-centre to a block makes the block rotate. A simulator that only moves the centre of mass gets that wrong. The lever-arm form includes the rotation for free.

14.4 Rest lengths: why "autoRest" exists

A spring's rest length L0 is the length at which it exerts no force. The user, however, draws the spring between two points, and usually means "this is relaxed as drawn". The program records L0 as the drawn length when the link is created and keeps updating it while the user edits the drawing (Model::refreshAutoRest), until the user types a different rest length (which sets autoRest = false: a preloaded spring). Distributions set autoRest = false and an explicit L0 equal to the gap between the faces.

The alternative, asking the user for the rest length of every spring, was rejected: the drawing is the specification, and a model that starts at rest should not have to be told so.

Chapter 15Generalizing the Spring: Distributions

A single spring cannot represent a bed of springs: a floor on elastic mounts, a machine on a row of isolators, a beam on an elastic foundation (a Winkler foundation). The user wanted to say "spread this much stiffness along that face, either evenly or growing along it". That is the distribution.

15.1 The mathematics: an integral turned into a sum

A distributed stiffness k(x) per metre along a span of length ℓ gives a total stiffness (for a pure translation of the object) and a stiffness-weighted centre:

K = ∫0ℓ k(x) dx, k(x) = k0 + m x (5.6)

For a constant distribution K = k0 ℓ. For the linear distribution that starts from zero (y = m x), K = m ℓ² / 2. The code replaces the integral with the midpoint rule:

K ≈ Σq=0N−1 k(xq) (ℓ/N), xq = (q + ½) ℓ / N (5.7)

and gives each of the N slices its own ordinary spring of stiffness k(xq) ℓ/N, placed at xq.

WHY?

Why the midpoint rule? For a function that is constant or straight (the two cases offered) the midpoint rule is exact, not an approximation. Σ (q + ½)/N × (1/N) = ½ for every N, so a linear distribution is reproduced to rounding error. The validation test in core_tests.cpp checks exactly this: 100 N/m total stiffness from a constant distribution and from a linear one, both vibrating a 4 kg block at √(K/m) = 5 rad/s to within 0.00000 m over three seconds.

One equivalent spring at the stiffness-weighted centroid Cannot resist rotation correctly: a block on a row of springs has a rotational stiffness ∫ k(x)(x − xc)² dx that one spring does not give. The rows of elements give it automatically through the lever arms.

A continuum finite-element foundation Needs a mesh of the contact face and a matrix assembly; the engine has no such machinery for contact faces. N independent springs reach the same answer for rigid faces.

  • The normal n. For a floor line the normal is the line's own perpendicular, flipped so that it points toward the object (dot(n, po - pg) < 0). For any other ground body it is the unit vector from the ground's centre to the object's centre.
  • The tangent t = (−ny, nx), the direction along the face. The span lives along t.
  • verts gathers the world-space corner points of a body (a circle gives its four extreme points, an anchor a short segment). Chapter 16Generalizing the Hinge: The Torsional Spring

A hinge (Pin) holds two points together and lets the bodies turn. A torsional spring adds a restoring moment about the hinge: it resists the relative angle θb − θa from a rest angle θ0.

Vt = ½ kt (θb − θa − θ0)², τb = − ∂Vt/∂θb = − kt (θb − θa − θ0), τa = −τb (5.8)

It is two lines in computeForces, in the hinge loop that already handles motors and rotary damping:

Making it a property of the Pin (a number kt, zero meaning a free hinge) means every place that already knows how to handle a hinge (the solver, the drawing, the file, the reaction readout) handles a torsional hinge for free. The "Torsional spring" tool in the Connections menu simply creates a Pin and sets kt.
  • Why reuse refAngle? The Weld already stores "the relative angle as drawn". The torsional spring wants exactly the same thing: it should rest at the angle the user drew. Model::refreshAutoRest updates refAngle for a Weld, and, since 1.2, for a Pin whose kt is not zero.
  • Both bodies. The moment is applied to B and the opposite moment to A, so the pair together exerts no net moment on the system (Newton's third law for moments). If A is the ground, its share is simply discarded.
  • Sign. Positive relative angle turns B clockwise on the screen, so a positive stretch gives a negative (counter-clockwise) moment: the spring pulls it back.

The validation test is the textbook one: a 3 kg beam pinned at one end, with I about the pin = Icm + m d² = 4.01 kg·m², released from 0.1 rad with kt = 40 N·m/rad, must cross zero after one quarter of the period π/2 · √(I/kt) = 0.4973 s. The program measures 0.4972 s.

Chapter 17Generalizing the Loads: Moments and F(t)

A load is a force or moment that does not come from another body. The program has gone through three generations of loads, each a small generalization of the previous one.

  1. A constant push or pull along a direction: loadN newtons, at loadAngleDeg, reversed if loadPull.
  2. A harmonic push, F cos ωt (the Drive): used for resonance studies.
  3. A moment and an expression of time (1.2): momentN and the formulas loadExpr and momentExpr evaluated at the current time.

All of them are added in World::externalForce and in the body loop of computeForces. The key to the third generation is that the magnitude can be any function of time, so the program needs a way to read a formula the user typed.

17.1 The expression evaluator

The formula "3t^3 + 2t^2 + 0t + 4" or "100sin(2pit)" is text typed at run time. The program cannot compile it into the C++ program, so it must interpret it. core/src/expr.cpp does this in two steps.

  1. Parsing. A recursive-descent parser turns the text into a tree of nodes stored in a flat array (std::vector<Node> nodes_). Each node is a number, a variable (x, L or t), a unary minus, a binary operator, or a function call. Precedence is encoded in the order of the parsing functions: sums call products, products call powers, powers call unary minus and atoms. The rule "^ binds tighter than unary minus" (so −x² means −(x²)) is what the textbook convention requires and what a user types.
  2. Evaluation. eval(x, L, t) walks the tree: one pass per call, no allocation. It is fast enough to be evaluated at every one of the 4000 steps a second the engine takes, for every body.

The parse is done once, when the world is built or when the user edits a formula (World::rebuildExprs); the interface caches parsed formulas by their text (Expr::cached) because it asks about the same formula every frame to draw the preview value.

WHY?

Why write a parser and not use a scripting language? A full language (Lua, Python, JavaScript) would let the user write anything, but (a) it brings a large dependency into a small, self-contained C++ program; (b) it can loop forever or crash; (c) it makes the validation suite harder to trust because behaviour could not be bounded. A formula language with only numbers, a few functions and no statements cannot hang and cannot do anything but compute a number from t. The cost is that the user cannot write a loop or an if. The functions step, sign, min, max and floor cover most of what people actually need (pulses, switches, saw-tooth waves).

Showing the load on the object. A load the user cannot see is a load the user forgets. In the drawing code (app/ui_canvas.cpp) every load draws an arrow from the body's centre and a label beside it. Power and the ledger. Every load does work, and the ledger needs it: P = F · v for a push and P = M ω for a moment. These are accumulated into pin and integrated by the trapezoid rule in World::step. That is why a time-varying load does not upset the energy balance: its work is counted at exactly the moment it is done.

Chapter 18Two Kinds of Connection: Smooth Forces and Constraints

So far every contribution has been a force: a spring pulls with a force that depends smoothly on positions and velocities. The equations of motion 5.3 can be integrated directly (the RK4 method below). A hinge is different. It does not exert a force that depends on position in any smooth way: it says "these two points shall be at the same place, always". Such a rule is a constraint.

18.1 Why constraints are hard

The obvious way to enforce a constraint is to treat it as an extremely stiff spring: if two points drift apart by d, apply a force 10⁹ · d to pull them back. This is the penalty method. It is easy to write and wrong for a general engine, for three reasons.

  1. It forces a tiny time step, because a stiff spring has a very high natural frequency, and the integrator must resolve it (the step must be much smaller than 1/ω).
  2. It never holds exactly. The points always separate a little, and the error grows if the system is heavily loaded.
  3. It adds energy storage that is not in the physical system, which pollutes the energy ledger.

The classical exact method is Lagrange multipliers: add unknown constraint forces λ, and solve the equations of motion together with the constraint equations, as one linear system, at each step. It is exact, but it needs a large matrix solve and becomes awkward when constraints can appear and disappear (every contact is a constraint that exists for a few milliseconds).

18.2 The choice: sequential impulses

The program uses sequential impulses, the method used by most real-time physics engines. The idea:

  1. Move all bodies by the smooth forces over the step, ignoring the constraints. Some constraints are now violated (a pin's two points have separated, or a ball is moving into the floor).
  2. For one constraint at a time, compute the impulse P (a change of momentum, in N·s) that would exactly fix that constraint's velocity error if it were the only one. Apply it.
  3. Fixing constraint 2 disturbs constraint 1. So repeat the loop over all the constraints a fixed number of times (14 by default, env.solverIterations). Each pass reduces the total error; for a stable arrangement it converges.

The impulse for a single constraint along a direction d comes straight from the effective inverse mass of equation 5.2. If the relative velocity along d is vrel and it should be zero:

P = − vrel / (ka + kb), ka = kDir(A, ra, d), kb = kDir(B, rb, d) (5.9)

Applying P at the points ra, rb changes the velocities by exactly the amount that cancels vrel. The same formula works for a contact normal, for a friction direction, for one of the two axes of a hinge, for a rod's axis. Only the direction d and the target change.

WHY?

Why not Lagrange multipliers? They would be exact in one solve, but they need a matrix assembled from every constraint at every step, and the matrix changes shape when a contact is made or broken. Sequential impulses need no matrix, handle a changing number of constraints trivially, and have a constant, small cost per constraint. The price is that a finite number of iterations leaves a small residual error, which the program controls with the iteration count and with the position correction below.

18.3 How locks, rails and fixed bodies come for free

Because the solver uses inverse masses and invMv gives zero along a locked axis, every constraint automatically respects every lock. A body on a Y rail is "infinitely heavy" in y: any impulse the solver wants to give it in y changes nothing. A static floor is infinitely heavy in both axes. No constraint needs to know about rails.

After each pass the function applyLocks also zeroes the locked velocity components explicitly, a belt-and-braces step that stops small numerical leakage.

18.4 Hinges, welds, rollers and rods

The four constraint kinds are four small functions inside World::solveJoints. Each is a variation of equation 5.9:

  • Pin. The constraint is two-dimensional: the two points must coincide in both x and y. The needed impulse is a 2-vector P solving the 2 × 2 system K P = −vrel, where the matrix K has entries k11, k12, k22 built from the inverse masses and lever arms (the code computes them and the determinant explicitly rather than calling a library). One impulse handles both axes, and it handles the coupling between them correctly.
  • Weld. A pin plus a third constraint on the angle: the relative angular velocity must be zero, with impulse λ = −ωrel / (1/Ia + 1/Ib).
  • Roller. One scalar constraint: the point must stay on a line, so the relative velocity along the line's normal is zeroed. The line is the support's x axis, rotated with its body.
  • Rod. One scalar constraint along the rod's own direction: the length must not change, which means the relative velocity along the rod is zero.

Each function accumulates the impulses it applies in jointImp_. Dividing by the step gives the reaction force at the joint, which is what the "reaction force at a support" readout shows.

18.5 Position drift and Baumgarte correction

A velocity-level fix cannot be perfect, and small position errors accumulate (a drift): after a thousand steps a pin's two points may have separated by a millimetre. The program removes the drift in a separate stage, correctPositions, run after the positions are advanced. It measures how far the constraint is violated and moves the bodies (not their velocities) by a fraction of that error, in proportion to their inverse masses:

Δpb = + β · e · (invMassb / (invMassa + invMassb)), β = 0.8 for joints, 0.2 for contacts (5.10)

For contacts the correction is limited to a small maximum per step (0.2 m) and ignores the first half-millimetre of overlap (kSlop = 0.0005 m). The slop is not laziness: a resting body must be allowed to sink in by a hair, otherwise the contact flickers on and off and the body jitters.

WHY?

Why not just remove the error from the velocity (Baumgarte stabilisation on velocities)? Adding a velocity proportional to the position error injects energy, which the ledger would flag. Correcting the positions directly, as the program does, adds only a tiny amount of potential energy; the ledger even tracks it (ledger_.posCorr) so that the user can see how much was added.

Chapter 19Contacts: Collisions, Friction and Rolling

Contacts are the most complex constraint, because they appear and disappear and because they have a direction-dependent law (they can push but never pull). Three stages: detection (do two bodies overlap?), manifold (where, and along which normal?) and solve (what impulses?).

19.1 Detection: three shape combinations

World::collide handles the three combinations. Circles are trivial (compare the distance with the sum of the radii). For polygons the program uses the Separating Axis Theorem (SAT): two convex shapes do not overlap if and only if there exists a line (an axis, one of the face normals) onto which their projections do not overlap. The function maxSeparation finds, for each face of one polygon, how far the other polygon lies outside that face; the largest such value, if positive, proves they are apart; if negative on every axis, it is the penetration depth on the axis of least penetration.

The face with the shallowest penetration is chosen as the reference face; a small bias (0.0005 m) prefers body A when two faces are almost tied, so that the choice does not flip every step and make the contact jitter. The other body's incident edge is the one most facing the reference face, and it is clipped to the reference face's side planes (clipSegment). The surviving end points become the contact points: up to two for polygon against polygon (an edge resting on an edge), one for circles.

WHY?

Why SAT and not GJK or a general distance routine? GJK handles any convex shape, but gives only one contact point per query, and a box resting on a floor needs two contact points to be stable (one point would let it rock). SAT with clipping gives the full resting manifold directly. Since the program's shapes are all convex polygons and circles (a beam is a thin box), SAT's limitation, convexity, is not a limitation here.

19.2 The manifold and warm starting

Each contact point becomes a Contact with the world points on each body, the normal (from A to B), the separation, and a key built from the body indices and the feature identifiers (which edge, which corner). The key lets the solver recognise "the same contact as the last step" and re-apply last step's impulse as a starting guess: warm starting. Resting stacks converge in one or two iterations instead of dozens, and nothing creeps. The cache is rebuilt each step (warm_) so that contacts that have ended are forgotten.

19.3 The impulses: normal, friction and rolling

In solveContacts, for each contact, in this order:

  1. Friction first, bounded by the previous pass's normal impulse. The tangent is t = (ny, −nx). The impulse that would cancel the sliding is clamped to the Coulomb cone: |Pt| ≤ μ Pn. If the clamp is active, the surfaces are sliding and the kinetic coefficient μk applies; otherwise they stick (static μs). Clamping an accumulated impulse, not each increment, is what makes the iterated friction law correct.
  2. The normal impulse, with the restitution target: λ = −mn (vn − bias), and the accumulated normal impulse is clamped at zero: contacts push, never pull. The bias is e · vn for a genuine impact (approach speed above restThreshold, 0.25 m/s by default) and zero otherwise, which is how a ball stops bouncing instead of micro-bouncing forever.
  3. Rolling resistance, an angular impulse that opposes relative spin, limited by (rollRes · R) Pn, applies only to circles.

The material properties of the pair are combined by the rule in the Environment (geometric mean for friction, maximum for restitution by default): World::combine.

19.4 Why a very small time step matters

Contacts are checked only at the start of a step. If a body moves further than its own thickness in one step it can jump straight through a wall: tunnelling. At the default step of 0.25 ms a body would have to move at more than 100 m/s to cross a 3 cm slab. The program adds a safety net in World::step: it measures how far every colliding body can move in the step (speed plus the speed the forces will add, plus rotation at the body's bounding radius) and, if that exceeds an eighth of the body's smallest dimension (a quarter of its half-width), divides the step into up to 64 sub-steps.

Chapter 20Advancing in Time

20.1 Two integrators, chosen automatically

World::usesConstraintSolver decides which of two methods advances the world:

  • RK4 (the classical fourth-order Runge-Kutta method, stepSmooth), for scenes with only smooth forces: springs, dampers, gravity, loads. It evaluates the forces four times per step and combines them with the weights 1, 2, 2, 1 over 6. Its error falls as the fifth power of the step, and the validation test shows the mass-spring-damper matches its exact solution to 8 × 10⁻¹³ m over 10 s. No constraint or contact is involved.
  • The constrained step (stepConstrained), as soon as there is a rod, pin, weld, roller, or two colliding bodies. It is semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler with the impulse solver in the middle, and its steps are:
  1. Compute the smooth forces and update the velocities: v ← v + (F/m) h.
  2. Apply the locks.
  3. Detect contacts and set up the impulses (warm starting).
  4. Run the solver: for 14 iterations, solveJoints, solveContacts, applyLocks.
  5. Do the energy accounting for the contacts (exact: ΔKE = P · (vbefore + vafter) / 2 for every impulse).
  6. Update the positions from the new velocities: p ← p + v h.
  7. Correct the position drift (correctPositions).
WHY?

Why change the method when constraints appear, rather than always using the constraint path? RK4 is far more accurate for smooth systems, and the simplest examples (a mass on a spring) are what a student checks against a formula. Using the less accurate method for them would degrade the result for no reason. The automatic choice also means that adding a hinge to a pure spring system silently changes the integrator, which is why the torsional-spring test, which contains a pin, was validated at the constrained step's accuracy (0.4972 s against 0.4973 s), not RK4's.

Why velocities before positions? Updating the velocity first and then using the new velocity to move the position is the semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler method. Unlike explicit Euler (position from the old velocity) it conserves energy over long runs for oscillating systems, with no systematic gain or loss. That is essential for a program that reports an energy ledger.

20.2 The energy ledger: the program checking itself

Every joule the program creates or destroys is recorded. The identity is

E(t) = E0 + Win − Dviscous − Ddrag − Dfriction − Dimpact + ε (5.11)

with E = T + V the kinetic plus potential energy, Win the work of the loads, the D terms the heat from each loss channel, and ε the numerical error, which is the only unexplained term. ledger_.numerical() returns ε. The validation suite fails the build if ε exceeds a small fraction of the energy that moved in any example.

The ledger is also how every generalization in this chapter was checked. When the distribution code was first written, the energy of ordinary springs jumped, and the ledger test failed: a copy-and-paste mistake used the wrong attachment point when a link was not a distribution. Without the ledger that error would have been invisible, because the motion still looked reasonable.

Chapter 22Recipe: Adding a New Element to the Engine

The torsional spring, the distributions and the time-varying loads were all added with the same recipe. Follow it for anything new (a rotational damper, a one-way spring, a gap element, a wind load).

  1. Write the physics on paper first. Give the energy (or the dissipation function, or the work) in terms of the state, and differentiate. Check the signs against the clockwise-positive, y-down convention. Predict a closed-form result you can test.
  2. Add the data to the model (model.hpp): as new fields on Link or Body with defaults that mean "off". A default of zero or empty means that old files and old code are unaffected.
  3. Add the force or constraint to the engine (world.cpp): a smooth force in computeForces and its energy in potentialEnergy; a constraint in solveJoints or solveContacts. Keep the force loop and the energy loop in step.
  4. Account for its power in the ledger. A load adds F·v or M·ω to the work-in integral; a dissipative element adds its heat to the right loss channel.
  5. Save and load it (project_io.cpp): append the new fields to the end of the body or link line and read them only if the line is long enough (f.size() > n). Files saved by older versions then open unchanged.
  6. Write the test in core_tests.cpp, build, and run vlab_tests.exe. A new element is not finished until the suite passes and the ledger test still passes.
  7. Add the interface: a tool or a field in the properties panel, how it is drawn, how it is picked (clicking it must select it), how it is deleted. The picking function (App::pickLink) was extended for distributions after a user could not select them; always test the click, not just the physics.
  8. Document it in the guide, and then publish (next chapter): rebuild, repackage, sync the Unreal plugin, commit and push.
TRY IT

Try it. Add a rotational damper to the hinge: a torque −cθ ωrel. Hint: the Pin already has a rotary damper, jointC. Read how computeForces uses it (the variable Tc), how the dissipation power is added to pv, and why the damper only acts when env.damping is on. Then write down which test would prove it correct (a hinged beam with only the rotary damper should decay as e−c t / 2I in angular velocity).

Chapter 24Updating and Editing the Suite

This chapter is the maintainer's manual. Two destinations, always. Chapter 27The Everyday Loop: Edit, Build, Test

Every change, large or small, goes round the same four-step loop. Do not skip steps to save time; the steps you skip are the ones that find the bug.

  1. Edit the source.
  2. Build: from the vlab-suite folder,
powershell
cmake --build build

The first time on a new machine, configure first: cmake -S . -B build -G Ninja -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release -DCMAKE_CXX_COMPILER=g++. After that cmake --build build compiles only the files that changed and relinks the four programs: the engine library, vlab_cli.exe, vlab_tests.exe and vlab_suite.exe (the window). 3. Test: .\build\vlab_tests.exe. It prints every check as PASS or FAIL with the measured and theoretical numbers, and ends with a line such as 92 of 92 checks passed. The exit code is 0 only if everything passed. 4. Look at it in the real program: run build\vlab_suite.exe. A passing test proves the physics; only your own eyes prove the click works.

WATCH OUT

Passing tests do not prove the interface works. Three of the bugs fixed in 1.2 were interface bugs that no test could see: a crash when a new connector was chosen (a list of names indexed past its end), a distribution that could not be clicked on, and a run time that kept coming back as 10 s because the saved last session restored it. Always exercise the thing you changed with the mouse.

27.1 Reading a build error

Compiler messages look hostile but follow a pattern: file, line, column, then the problem. Read the first error only; the later ones are usually consequences. A real example from this project:

app/ui_canvas.cpp:784:32: error: use of deleted function 'void ImDrawList::AddPolyline(...)'

The cause was a drawing call whose argument types matched a deliberately disabled overload in the library. The fix (drawing the curve as a series of short lines) took one edit. The habit to learn is: go to the line, read the message, change one thing, rebuild.

27.2 Reproduce before you fix

When something behaves wrongly, do not edit the engine first. Write a small standalone program in a scratch folder that builds the model by hand and prints the numbers. Compile it against the engine with one command:

powershell
g++ -std=c++17 -O2 -Icore/include repro.cpp (Get-ChildItem core/src/*.cpp).FullName -o repro.exe

Because the engine has no window code, this works for any physics question. The 1.2 collision work began exactly this way: a program that drove a block into a wall at different forces, printed where it ended up, and showed that the engine's behaviour at ordinary forces was right and that tunnelling happened only at absurd speeds. Without that program the temptation would have been to rewrite the contact solver, which was not at fault.

28.1 Save-file compatibility: the rule of appending

Files are plain text, one line per body and per link, with fields separated by tabs. The loader reads field n by position. The rule that has kept every old file working is therefore:

KEY WORD

Only ever append. A new field goes at the end of the line, and the loader reads it only if the line is long enough (if (f.size() > 49) ...), otherwise it leaves the default. Never insert a field in the middle and never reorder: every later field would shift, and old files would silently load wrong numbers.

The same rule explains a subtle behaviour: a design saved with a 10 s run time keeps 10 s when reopened, because the file stored a value. Changing a default in the code changes only designs that have no stored value.

Chapter 29Packaging: What the Release Script Does

tools/package.py turns the working tree into the thing people run. Run it from anywhere:

powershell
$env:PATH = "C:\msys64\mingw64\bin;$env:PATH"
python tools\package.py

It performs these steps in order, and stops at the first failure:

  1. Configure and build with CMake and Ninja in Release mode (-O2). This always rebuilds anything stale, so the package can never contain an old binary.
  2. Run the validation suite. If any check fails the script prints "validation suite FAILED: not packaging" and exits. This is a gate: a build that fails its physics tests cannot be released by accident. The suite's output is saved as Documents\validation_report.txt inside the package, so the report that ships is the report of the exact binary that ships.
  3. Copy the programs: the window (VibrationLabSuite.exe), the command-line runner and the test runner (into Tools), with debug symbols stripped to save space.
  4. Write every built-in example as a .vlab file into Examples, by asking the command-line runner to save each one. The examples therefore always match the code that defines them.
  5. Check the Excel export on a fresh run (the structure always; the charts too if the Python library openpyxl is installed).
  6. Copy the documents: the user guide PDF, the README and the third-party licences.
  7. Copy the Unreal plugin from ue5.
  8. Zip everything into VibrationLabSuite-1.2-win64.zip.

The last two lines it prints name the zip and the executable. If you do not see them, the package was not produced.

29.1 The version number

The version lives in two places that must agree: VERSION = "1.2" near the top of tools/package.py (it names the zip), and the cover text of the guide. Change both when you start a new release.

29.2 The safety copy: VLAB_DIST

By default the package is written to dist. Setting the environment variable VLAB_DIST to another folder writes it there instead:

powershell
$env:VLAB_DIST = "$PWD\dist-1.2"
python tools\package.py
Remove-Item Env:VLAB_DIST

During 1.2 the script was run twice per change: once for dist (committed) and once for dist-1.2 (a convenient local copy to run from while dist is in use). Remember to remove the variable afterwards, or the next run will go to the wrong folder.

29.3 The failure that teaches the most: the locked executable

If the program is running from dist while you package, Windows refuses to overwrite the running executable, and the script stops with:

PermissionError: [Errno 13] Permission denied: '...\dist\VibrationLabSuite\VibrationLabSuite.exe'

The script deletes the old package folder before copying the new files, so a failure at this point leaves dist half rebuilt: the README and tools gone, the old executable still there (locked). This happened in 1.2, and the half-built folder was committed by mistake with git add -u, which stages deletions as well as changes. The repository briefly held a broken package. The recovery was:

powershell
git checkout <last-good-commit> -- dist     # restore the previous complete package
git commit -m "Restore dist/ after an interrupted packaging run"
git push

and then, with the program closed, running the packaging script again and committing the result.

WATCH OUT

Before packaging, close every copy of VibrationLabSuite. Check with Get-Process | Where-Object { $_.ProcessName -like "VibrationLab*" }. If nothing is listed, packaging is safe. Never kill someone else's running window to make it work; ask them to close it.

Chapter 30Keeping the Unreal Plugin in Step

The Unreal plugin does not link to the engine in core. It contains its own copy of the engine's source files, so that it can be built by Unreal's own build system. After any change to a file in core/include or core/src, run:

powershell
python ue5\sync_core.py

It deletes the plugin's copy and copies the current engine in. Then commit the changed files under ue5/ together with the engine change. If you forget, the desktop program and the Unreal plugin silently drift apart: the same model would behave differently in the two. The check is simple: after syncing, git status should show changes under ue5/VLab/Source/VLabRuntime/ThirdParty that mirror the changes under core.

Chapter 31Git: Committing and Pushing

31.1 The state of the repository

Work happens on a branch (during 1.2 the branch was v1.2-deformation); the stable line is main. The branch has a remote called origin, the copy on GitHub. Four commands tell you where you are:

Command Tells you

31.2 What to stage, and what never to

  • Stage with git add -u to include every already tracked file you modified or deleted (source, tests, and the files under dist). It never adds new files by accident, which is why it was used for 1.2.
  • Add a new file explicitly: git add path\to\file. A new source file, a new test, a new chapter must be added by name, or it will not be in the commit.
  • Never commit build/ (it is in .gitignore), scratch programs, or dist-1.2 (left untracked on purpose; git status shows it as ??).
  • Read git status before every commit. An unexpected D (deleted) line is how the half-built dist was nearly published.

Why is dist in the repository at all? Because the repository is also the place people download the finished program from. The cost is that every release makes large binary changes. The alternative, publishing zips as separate release downloads, keeps the repository small but needs a second step and a second place to look. The project chose one place.

powershell

On Windows Git prints many lines such as warning: LF will be replaced by CRLF the next time Git touches it. They are about how line endings are stored and are harmless here. They are noisy, not errors; the real errors start with error: or fatal:.

31.6 Merging to main

When the work on a branch is finished and tested, it is merged into main, normally through a pull request on GitHub (so that the change is reviewed and the history is clear). From the branch: git push, then open the pull request from v1.2-deformation into main. After the merge, a tag marks the release (git tag v1.2 then git push origin v1.2), so that the exact source of every release can be found later. These two steps were not needed during development of 1.2, which all happened on one branch, but are the normal route to a public release.
