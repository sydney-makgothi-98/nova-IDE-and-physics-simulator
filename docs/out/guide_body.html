Welcome

What Vibration Lab Suite does

Vibration Lab Suite is a sandbox for mechanical motion. You draw a system on a canvas: balls, blocks and polygons, floor lines and ramps with their own friction, beams with pinned, roller or cantilever supports, springs, dampers, rods and hinges with motors. You choose how realistic the physics should be. Then you run it for as long as you like and watch it move, with live numbers and plots beside it.

When you are done, one button turns the whole run into an Excel workbook: every number, with units, plus charts that are already drawn. A second button records an .mp4 video.

It is built for three kinds of people:

  • Students of dynamics and vibrations, who want to see what the equations say and check their homework against a simulation.
  • Engineers who want a quick what-if model before they commit to heavy software.
  • Teachers and developers who want convincing, correct motion for demonstrations, courses and games.

Installing

There is nothing to install. Double-click VibrationLabSuite.exe. The program opens straight into the design screen with a worked example loaded.

NOTE

You need Windows 10 or 11 (64-bit) and a graphics card that supports Direct3D 11 (every PC of the last ten years does). Excel is not needed to run the program or to create the workbook. You only need Excel (or LibreOffice, Google Sheets, Numbers) to open the workbook afterwards.

Turning an object before you place it

While a placement tool is active, a ghost follows the cursor. Press Q or E (or hold Ctrl and turn the mouse wheel) to rotate it in steps before you click, or type an angle in Rotation in the Library. The same rotation is used for floors (ramps and walls), beams and the direction of a roller support.

Before you click, the Settings for the next one block lets you choose the size, radius, mass and material. Things snap to a grid (0.1 m by default; hold Alt to ignore it).

1.2 Sizes and radii

Everything is measured in metres, kilograms and seconds. After placing something, select it and type exact numbers in Properties:

  • a ball has a radius;
  • a block has a width and a height;
  • a polygon has a number of sides and a radius (the distance from its centre to a corner);
  • a wedge has two leg lengths;
  • a beam has a length and a thickness, and its mass comes from the material's density;
  • a floor is a line: it has only a length and an angle.

Mass can be typed directly (kilograms) or worked out from a density and the shape's area. The computed mass and moment of inertia are shown underneath.

KEY WORD

Depth. This is a 2-D simulator. Every body is treated as 1 metre deep, so "density x area" gives its mass. If you only ever type the mass directly, depth never matters.

1.3 Floors and their friction

A floor is a line with hatching on the side it supports and no thickness: it is ground, not an object, so it can never be hinged, bent or knocked over. It has a material, and therefore friction and bounce. Place one, then select it: Properties shows its length, angle and surface material. The material sets its friction and bounciness. Give each floor its own material to compare surfaces: for example, put an ice floor and a rubber floor side by side and slide the same block across both.

1.4 Beams and supports

A beam is a heavy rigid body. Drop it with nothing under it and it falls. To hold it up, choose a support and click the beam: the support snaps to the beam's ends, the middle of its edges and its centre.

Support Holds Reaction shown
Pin Position. The beam may rotate. A force (x and y).
Roller One direction only (up, for a flat roller). The beam may slide and rotate. A force perpendicular to the roller's axis.
Fixed (cantilever) Position and angle. A force and a moment.

While the model runs, click a support symbol and Properties shows the reaction force (and moment, for a fixed support); an orange arrow is drawn at the support. Try the examples Simply supported beam (the two reactions share the load) and Cantilever with a tip load (moment = weight x distance from the wall). {{img:suite-support.png|A running simply supported beam: the orange arrows are the two support reactions, each carrying half the weight.}}

A pin can also carry a motor: a constant torque, a sinusoidal torque and rotary damping, all in Properties. The Robotic arm example is built from these.

1.5 Springs, dampers, rods and pins

Click a connection tool, click one body, then click another. Click near a body's centre to attach to its centre; click elsewhere on it to attach at that spot (this makes it twist as well as move).

  • A spring is born relaxed at the length you drew it. Changing its rest length in Properties pre-stretches or pre-compresses it. A positive cubic stiffness makes it harder the more it stretches.
  • A damper resists speed: force = c x (speed of stretching). It turns motion into heat. It is the only thing that dissipates energy in a pure spring-mass system.
  • A rod holds two points at a fixed distance.
  • A pin makes two bodies share one point. A pendulum is an anchor, a rod and a ball.
  • A fixed (weld) joint makes two bodies share one point and one angle: they move as one piece.

Springs change colour while the model runs: warm when they pull, cool when they push.

1.7 Materials

The built-in materials give typical textbook values. They are a starting point: type your own numbers for any surface.

Material Static friction Kinetic friction Bounciness (e) Rolling resistance
Ideal 0 0 1.00 0

Aluminium 0.61 0.47 0.60 0.003
Rubber 1.00 0.80 0.80 0.020
Wood 0.50 0.35 0.50 0.010

When two bodies touch, their friction is combined from both surfaces (by default, the geometric mean: the square root of the product). Bounciness takes the larger of the two. You can change both rules under Environment, Advanced.

NOTE

Static versus kinetic friction. Static friction decides whether a resting body starts to slide: it slides only when the push along the surface exceeds the static friction times the pressing force. Once sliding, the lower kinetic friction applies. That is why a block on a ramp can stay put at one angle and run away at a slightly steeper one.

1.8 Undo, save, open

Ctrl+Z and Ctrl+Y undo and redo every edit (a slider drag counts as one step). Ctrl+S saves the design as a .vlab file; Ctrl+O opens one. The program also remembers your last design automatically.

Chapter 2Ideal and Realistic Physics

The Environment panel has two big buttons and a list of switches.

Switch What it controls
Ideal One press turns friction, inelastic impacts, dampers, rolling resistance and drag all off. Nothing can lose energy.
Realistic Turns friction, inelastic impacts, dampers and rolling resistance on, using the materials.
Collisions between bodies Off: bodies pass through each other.
Friction Off: every surface is slippery (friction = 0).
Inelastic impacts Off: every collision is perfectly elastic (e = 1).
Rolling resistance Off: a ball rolls forever on a flat floor.
Dampers active Off: dampers are ignored.
Air drag A force against velocity: F = -(c1 + c2 |v|) v. You choose c1 and c2.
Gravity Pull toward the bottom of the screen. Presets for Earth, Moon and zero gravity.

You can mix them: for example, friction on but bouncing perfectly elastic, to isolate the effect of friction alone.

2.1 What changes when you switch

TRY IT

Load Bouncing ball: ideal vs realistic. Run it in Realistic mode: each bounce is lower than the last and the ball settles on the floor. Now press Ideal and run again: the ball returns to its starting height every time, forever. Open Telemetry, Energy ledger to see the difference in where the energy goes.

TRY IT

Load Friction on an incline: four materials and run it. Rubber and steel stay put; wood and ice slide. Now press Ideal (no friction) and run again: all four slide down together with the same acceleration, g sin(angle).

2.2 The energy ledger

Energy is never created or destroyed; it only changes form. The program keeps a running account and shows it in the Energy ledger tab:

  • Work in: energy fed in by pushes and by you dragging with the mouse.
  • Dampers: heat from dampers.
  • Air drag: heat from drag.
  • Friction: heat from sliding and from rolling resistance.
  • Impacts: kinetic energy lost in collisions.

The law of energy says E(end) = E(start) + work in - energy lost. The tab prints both sides and the difference, called the numerical error. That number is the program checking itself: it should be tiny (a few thousandths of a joule at most). If it ever grows, something is wrong, and that is useful to know.

WHY?

Why does this matter? Many simulators just show motion; you cannot tell if the motion is right. A ledger that balances is strong evidence that the forces and the time stepping are correct, and it teaches the central idea of mechanics: the energy goes somewhere.

Chapter 3Running the Model

3.1 How long to run

The Run panel starts with the question How long should the model run? Type any number of seconds from a fraction of a second to an hour, or press 5 s, 10 s, 30 s, 1 min, 5 min. The model stops by itself when the time is up.

Underneath, Data rows per second sets how often the program records a row of data. Higher rates give smoother data and bigger Excel files. A good starting point is 100 rows per second; for a 5-minute run use 20 to 50. The panel tells you how many rows to expect. (A workbook can hold a little over one million rows, so very long runs are automatically thinned to fit.)

3.2 Two ways to run

Button What happens Use it when
Run Plays in real time. You can pause, drag bodies with the mouse, and change sliders while it runs. You want to watch and play with it.
Compute the whole run now (fast) Runs the full duration as fast as the computer can and shows all the data at once. You only need the numbers: a 5-minute run takes about a second.

Other controls: Pause/Resume (Space), Stop (Esc) returns to design mode, Step 1/60 s advances one frame while paused, and the real-time speed slider runs from 0.1x (slow motion) to 4x.

TIP

Push things around. While a run is playing, press the mouse on a moving body and drag. A soft invisible rubber band pulls it toward the cursor, and the energy you put in appears in the ledger as "work in". Let go and watch how the system recovers.

3.3 Changing things while it runs

Sliders and number fields in Properties and Environment (stiffness, damping, friction, mass, gravity, even ideal/realistic) take effect immediately in a running model. Changes to the layout (adding, deleting, moving things) need you to press Stop first. Press Stop and your data from the last run is still there to plot and export.

3.4 Recording a video

Press Record video (F9) at any time, press it again to finish. The video shows the canvas (or the whole window if you tick the box) and is saved as an .mp4 in Documents\Vibration Lab Suite\Recordings.

While recording, every video frame is exactly 1/60 of a second of physics, so the video plays at the correct speed even if your computer was busy while recording. The program uses the video encoder that is already part of Windows; nothing else needs installing.

Chapter 4Telemetry and Excel

4.1 What is recorded

For every moving body, at every sample: its position (x, y), its displacement from the start (dx, dy), angle, velocity (vx, vy), speed, spin and kinetic energy. For every spring: length, stretch and force. For every damper: length, stretching rate, force and the heat power it is producing. For the whole system: kinetic, potential and total energy, the energy ledger, and the number of contacts.

4.2 In the program

The Telemetry panel has seven tabs:

  • Plots - tick any channels and plot them against time. Scroll to zoom, drag to pan, double-click to fit. Quick-pick buttons choose positions, velocities, energy, forces or angles.
  • Energy ledger - where every joule went (see the previous chapter).
  • Path and phase - the path of a body on the canvas, and its phase portrait (velocity against displacement).
  • Spectrum - the frequency content of any signal.
  • Statistics - minimum, maximum, mean, RMS and final value of every channel.
  • Modal analysis - natural frequencies and mode shapes.
  • Frequency response - a resonance curve.

4.3 Exporting to Excel

Press Export to Excel (.xlsx) with charts (or Ctrl+E), choose a name and a folder. The workbook is a normal Excel file with these sheets:

Sheet What is in it
Summary Your whole setup in words and numbers (so the report explains itself): run settings, environment, every body with its shape, mass and material, every connection, the final energy ledger, and statistics of every channel.
Telemetry One row per sample, one column per channel, with units in the headings. The first row is frozen so the headings stay visible.
Charts Ready-made charts: displacement, velocity, rotation angle, energy, the energy ledger, forces and damper power. For runs longer than 20 seconds, an extra chart shows the first 10 seconds in detail.
Spectrum, Modal, FrequencyResponse Included automatically if you computed them before exporting.

The charts are real Excel charts that stay linked to the Telemetry sheet. Edit the data and they update; right-click to restyle them.

Making your own chart

On the Telemetry sheet, select the Time column and the channel you want (hold Ctrl to select non-adjacent columns), then Insert, Scatter with Straight Lines. Every channel is a column; every row is a moment.

CSV

Export CSV makes a plain table that any program can read (Excel, Python, MATLAB, R). The file starts with a marker so that Excel shows the units correctly.

TIP

For homework and lab reports: export the workbook, copy the charts you need into your report, and keep the Summary sheet: it records exactly which design produced the numbers.

Chapter 5Worked Tutorials

5.1 1. Natural frequency and damping of a mass on a spring

Load Mass-spring-damper (1 degree of freedom) (mass 2 kg, spring 30 N/m, damper 1.5 N s/m, released from 1 m stretch).

  1. Press Compute the whole run now (fast).
  2. Open Telemetry, Spectrum, choose the channel Mass dx, press Compute spectrum. The peak reads 0.61 Hz; the tab also prints the damping ratio estimated from the decay: 0.097.
  3. Theory: natural frequency sqrt(k/m) / (2 pi) = sqrt(15) / (2 pi) = 0.616 Hz. Damping ratio = c / (2 sqrt(k m)) = 1.5 / (2 sqrt(60)) = 0.097.
  4. Now double the spring to 60 N/m in Properties and repeat: the frequency should rise by a factor of about 1.41.

5.2 2. Resonance

Same system, switched to zero gravity. Select the mass and set a harmonic push: 5 N, direction 0, frequency 3.9 rad/s (the natural frequency). Run for 30 seconds and look at Plots and the Energy ledger: the amplitude grows until the energy fed in each cycle equals the heat the damper removes. Now set the push frequency to 8 rad/s: the same push barely moves it. Switch dampers off (press Ideal) at resonance and the amplitude grows without limit.

5.3 3. Which blocks slide?

Load Friction on an incline: four materials. The ramp is 28 degrees, so tan(28 degrees) = 0.53. A block slides only when the static friction between it and the ramp is below 0.53. With a wooden ramp the combined values are rubber 0.71, steel 0.61, wood 0.50, ice 0.22, so rubber and steel stay and wood and ice slide. Run it and confirm, then check the Energy ledger: the "friction" bar is the heat from the two sliding blocks.

5.4 4. Bounce and restitution

Load Bouncing ball: ideal vs realistic. A rubber ball (e = 0.8) dropped from 2 m should rebound to e squared x 2 = 1.28 m, then 0.82 m, and so on. Read the peaks from the Plots tab (channel Ball dy) and compare. The ledger's impacts bar after the first bounce is m g h (1 - e squared) = 3.5 J for the default 0.5 kg ball.

5.5 5. A tuned mass damper

Load Tuned mass damper: a 10 kg main mass on a spring, pushed at its resonance, with a small 1 kg absorber attached by its own spring and damper.

  1. Run 40 seconds and look at Plots: the main mass moves very little because the absorber takes the motion.
  2. Open Frequency response and press Run frequency sweep: instead of one tall resonance peak you see two small ones either side. That split peak is the signature of a tuned absorber.
  3. Open Modal analysis and press the button: the two natural frequencies match the two peaks.

Chapter 6Analysis Tools

6.1 Spectrum

Choose a channel and press Compute spectrum. The program shows how strongly each frequency is present in that signal and marks the strongest. It removes the average first (so a constant offset is not mistaken for a very slow wave). For a damped oscillation the peak sits at the damped frequency.

6.2 Modal analysis

A system with several moving parts can vibrate in several modes. Each has its own natural frequency and a shape (who moves with whom). Press Compute natural frequencies and mode shapes and click a mode: in design mode it is animated on the canvas.

The analysis linearises the springs and dampers about the drawn pose; contacts and joints are ignored. A mode with 0 Hz means part of the system is not held by any spring and can drift.

6.3 Frequency response

Choose where to push (the input), where to measure (the output), a range of frequencies, and press Run frequency sweep. For each frequency the program runs a full steady-state simulation and reports the amplitude and the phase lag of the response. The result is the resonance curve of the system. Each frequency is a real simulation, so a sweep takes a few seconds to a minute.

7.1 Honest limits

  • It is a 2-D simulator. Bodies move in a plane and have a depth of 1 m.
  • The material values are generic textbook numbers. Real friction depends on the surface, the speed and the temperature: measure yours for engineering decisions.
  • Contacts are solved with a sequential-impulse method with a very small time step. Very high stacks, very fast thin objects and very stiff, very light parts can still misbehave; if the numbers blow up the program stops and tells you.
  • It is validated against theory, but it is not a certified engineering tool.

Chapter 8Troubleshooting and Questions

Problem What to do
"The numbers blew up" message A very light body on a very stiff spring, or a huge force. Raise the mass, soften the spring, or reduce the physics step (Environment, Advanced).
"Press Stop first" You tried to add, delete or move something while a run exists. Press Stop; sliders and fields still work during a run.
Nothing happens when I press Run There is no moving body. Place a ball, block or polygon.
A body falls through the floor Make sure both have "Collides with other bodies" ticked, collisions are on in the Environment, and the floor line is long enough to reach the body.
The ball never stops bouncing You are in Ideal mode, or the restitution is 1. Press Realistic.
The block slides although I expect it to stick Static friction is below tan(angle). The combined friction is the geometric mean of the two surfaces.
"Export failed" The file is probably open in Excel. Close it or choose another name.
The video does not appear It is in Documents\Vibration Lab Suite\Recordings. If the window was made smaller during recording, the program stops it safely.
The layout is a mess View, Reset window layout.

9.2 Conventions

  • Units: metres, kilograms, seconds, newtons, joules, radians per second.
  • y points down, like the screen. Gravity pulls toward positive y. An angle of 0 points right; positive angles turn clockwise on the screen.
  • A body's dx, dy are its displacement from where it started; x, y are absolute.
